What Are Large Reasoning Models (LRMs)?

LRMs are a new generation of AI models designed to "think" before answering. Unlike traditional language models that respond immediately, LRMs generate detailed reasoning chains - often called "thinking traces" - before providing their final answer.

lightbulb Think of It Like...

Imagine two students taking a math test. A regular LLM is like a student who blurts out answers immediately - sometimes right, sometimes wrong. An LRM is like a student who shows their work, writing out each step of their reasoning. The promise is that this "thinking out loud" leads to better answers... but does it really?

LRMs are trained using reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards - they learn to produce better reasoning traces by getting feedback on whether their final answers are correct. This training encourages self-reflection, backtracking, and exploring multiple solution paths.

science

The Clever Methodology

The researchers realized that testing on standard math benchmarks has problems: (1) models may have seen similar problems during training, and (2) you can't precisely control difficulty. So they used classic puzzles instead.

Why Puzzles Work Better

Controllable complexity: Add more disks to Tower of Hanoi = exponentially harder. Add more checkers = quadratically harder. This lets researchers see exactly when and how models fail.

The Four Puzzle Environments

stacked_bar_chart Tower of Hanoi

Move all disks from peg A to peg C, never placing larger disks on smaller ones.

Complexity: 2^N - 1 moves

swap_horiz Checker Jumping

Swap positions of red and blue checkers by sliding or jumping.

Complexity: (N+1)^2 - 1 moves

directions_boat River Crossing

Transport actors and agents across a river with safety constraints.

Complexity: ~Linear with N

view_in_ar Blocks World

Rearrange stacked blocks from initial to goal configuration.

Complexity: ~Linear with N

The Secret Weapon: Simulators

Unlike math problems where you only know if the final answer is right, the researchers built simulators that could:

  • Verify each individual move in a solution
  • Identify exactly where the model first made a mistake
  • Extract and evaluate intermediate solutions from the thinking traces
  • Track when correct vs. incorrect solutions appeared during "thinking"
insights

The Three Regimes of Complexity

The central finding: there are three distinct "regimes" based on problem complexity, and thinking models don't always win!

Low Complexity

Winner: Standard LLMs

Simple problems where extra "thinking" is wasteful overhead. Standard models solve them faster and more efficiently.

Medium Complexity

Winner: Thinking Models

The sweet spot where extended reasoning actually helps. LRMs explore possibilities and self-correct.

High Complexity

Winner: Nobody (0% accuracy)

Both model types completely collapse. Thinking doesn't help - performance goes to zero.

lightbulb The Race Car Analogy

Imagine two cars: a sports car (standard LLM) and a thinking car that stops to consult GPS at every intersection (LRM). For a trip to the corner store, the sports car wins - no thinking needed. For a cross-city journey, the GPS car finds better routes. But for an impossible journey across an ocean? Both cars fail completely - the GPS just wastes more time before giving up.

warning The Uncomfortable Truth

LRMs don't "think better" - they just delay failure. At high complexity, the advantage of thinking completely disappears. The extra reasoning tokens are essentially wasted compute.

The Token Budget Paradox

Here's what makes this truly puzzling: these models have large token budgets (64,000 tokens) that they're nowhere close to exhausting. They could keep thinking, but they choose to reduce their reasoning effort right when problems get hardest.

What This Suggests: The models seem to "recognize" when they're in over their heads and essentially give up. This isn't graceful degradation - it's a fundamental scaling limit in their reasoning architecture.

How Thinking Patterns Change with Complexity

sentiment_satisfied Low Complexity

  • Correct solution found early
  • Model keeps exploring anyway
  • Wasted compute on "overthinking"
  • Usually returns to correct answer

sentiment_dissatisfied Medium/High Complexity

  • Many incorrect attempts first
  • Correct solution (if any) found late
  • Model may fixate on early wrong answer
  • Often never finds correct solution
lightbulb Self-Correction is Limited

LRMs do have some self-correction ability - that's why they outperform standard models at medium complexity. But this ability doesn't scale. Past a threshold, models fixate on wrong answers and can't backtrack effectively.

code

The Shocking Algorithm Test

The researchers ran a revealing experiment: what if we give the model the exact algorithm to solve Tower of Hanoi? Surely then it can execute the steps correctly?

Result: No improvement! Even with the complete recursive algorithm provided in the prompt, models still collapsed at roughly the same complexity threshold. They couldn't reliably execute prescribed logical steps.

This is significant because:

  • Finding a solution requires search, creativity, and exploration
  • Executing a known algorithm should be much easier - just follow the steps!
  • Yet models failed at both equally
lightbulb Like a Chef Who Can't Follow Recipes

Imagine a chef who can sometimes invent creative dishes, but when given a detailed recipe, still burns the food at step 47. The limitation isn't creativity - it's the fundamental ability to track state and execute sequential operations consistently.

The Training Data Hypothesis

Another puzzling finding: models behave very differently across puzzle types, even with similar numbers of moves required.

First Error Position by Puzzle Type

Tower of Hanoi
~100 correct moves before error
River Crossing
~4 correct moves before error

Models can execute ~100 correct moves in Tower of Hanoi but fail after just ~4 moves in River Crossing. The likely explanation? Tower of Hanoi is all over the internet; River Crossing with N>2 is rare. These models aren't reasoning - they're pattern matching against training data.

lightbulb

What This Means

For AI Researchers

Current LRM architectures may have fundamental barriers to generalizable reasoning. Simply adding more "thinking" through longer chains-of-thought won't solve the problem. New approaches to training and architecture may be needed.

For Practitioners

check_circle When to Use LRMs

  • Medium-complexity reasoning tasks
  • Problems similar to training data
  • When you need visible reasoning traces
  • Tasks benefiting from self-correction

cancel When NOT to Use LRMs

  • Very simple tasks (wasteful)
  • High-complexity planning (will fail)
  • Novel problem structures
  • Tasks requiring exact computation

For Everyone

psychology_alt The Question of "Real" Reasoning

Is the "thinking" in LRMs genuine reasoning, or sophisticated pattern matching that looks like reasoning? When models fail at well-defined puzzles but succeed on training-data-adjacent problems, it suggests the latter. True reasoning would generalize.

Glossary

Large Reasoning Model (LRM)
AI models that generate extended "thinking traces" before answering, trained to reason step-by-step. Examples: OpenAI o1/o3, DeepSeek-R1, Claude 3.7 Thinking.
Thinking Tokens
The tokens generated during the model's internal reasoning process before it produces a final answer. These represent the model's "thought process."
Chain-of-Thought (CoT)
A prompting technique where models break down problems into intermediate steps. LRMs are trained to produce these automatically.
Compositional Depth
The number of sequential operations (moves) required to solve a puzzle. Higher depth = more complexity.
Overthinking
When a model finds a correct solution early but continues generating incorrect alternatives, wasting compute on unnecessary exploration.
Accuracy Collapse
The phenomenon where model performance drops to 0% beyond a certain complexity threshold, with no gradual degradation.
pass@k
A metric measuring the probability that at least one of k generated samples is correct. Used to evaluate upper-bound capabilities.
Reinforcement Learning (RL)
Training method where models learn from feedback on their outputs. LRMs use RL with verifiable rewards to learn better reasoning.

Key Takeaways

  • insights
    Three Regimes Exist: Standard LLMs win at low complexity (more efficient), LRMs win at medium complexity (thinking helps), both fail completely at high complexity (0% accuracy).
  • trending_down
    Counterintuitive Scaling: As problems get harder, LRMs initially think more, then mysteriously reduce their reasoning effort right before complete collapse - even with unused token budget.
  • loop
    Overthinking is Real: For simple problems, models often find correct answers early but waste compute exploring wrong alternatives. Self-correction exists but doesn't scale.
  • code_off
    Can't Execute Algorithms: Even when given explicit algorithms, models fail at similar complexity thresholds. The limitation is fundamental, not about strategy discovery.
  • pattern
    Pattern Matching, Not Reasoning: Models perform vastly better on Tower of Hanoi (common online) than River Crossing (rare), suggesting reliance on training data rather than true reasoning.
  • warning
    Fundamental Barriers: Current LRM approaches may have inherent limitations. More "thinking" alone won't solve the problem - new architectures or training methods may be needed.
